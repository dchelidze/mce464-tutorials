The Energy Landscape and Linearization

A one-DOF system is a ball rolling in a potential well. Equilibria are the flat spots, tangent stiffness is the curvature there, and linearization replaces the well by its osculating parabola. See where that replacement works and where it fails.

1. Reading the potential

For a conservative system with coordinate \(x\), energy conservation reads \(\tfrac12 m\dot x^2+V(x)=E\), and the equation of motion is \(m\ddot x=-V'(x)\). Three geometric facts:

  • Equilibrium is a point where the slope vanishes: \(V'(x_e)=0\).
  • Tangent stiffness is the curvature there: \(k_T=V''(x_e)\). Positive curvature is a well (stable), negative curvature is a hilltop (unstable).
  • Linearization replaces \(V\) by \(\tfrac12k_T(x-x_e)^2\). The natural frequency is \(\omega=\sqrt{k_T/m}\); here \(m=1\).

With a constant load (Lecture 4) the well keeps its shape but slides: the equilibrium moves to \(x_s\), and the dynamic coordinate \(y=x-x_s\) removes the load from the equation entirely.

(4) Load case: the linear model is exact, and the only thing the load does is move the equilibrium.

2. Why the pendulum period grows

For the pendulum the exact period is \(T=4K(k)/\omega_0\) with \(k=\sin(\theta_{\max}/2)\), so \(T/T_0\approx 1+\theta_{\max}^2/16\) for small amplitude. The well is flatter than its parabola: the potential bends below the dashed curve, the restoring force is weaker than the linear one, and each swing takes longer. A well that stiffens with amplitude (hardening) would do the reverse, and a double well shows both at once.

3. Check yourself

Where does the tangent stiffness of an inverted pendulum come from, and what is its sign?

\(V=mg\ell\cos\theta\) near \(\theta=0\) has \(V''=-mg\ell<0\): a hilltop. The growth rate of small deviations is \(\sqrt{g/\ell}\), the exponent of \(e^{\pm\lambda t}\) replacing the oscillating \(e^{\pm i\omega t}\).

Under a constant load, why does the vibration frequency not change?

A constant force tilts the potential by a linear term, \(V\to V-Fx\). The curvature \(V''\) is untouched, so the shape of the well and \(\omega\) are the same. Only the minimum moves, \(x_s=F/k\).

If two initial conditions have the same energy, do they have the same period?

In a one-DOF conservative system, yes: the orbit is the same closed contour in the phase plane. Different energy means a different contour and, for a nonlinear well, a different period.
