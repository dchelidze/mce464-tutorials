Phase Portraits and the Trace–Determinant Plane

Two numbers, the trace and the determinant of the state matrix, decide the geometry of every linear equilibrium. Drag a point through the trace–determinant plane and watch the whole phase portrait flow into a new shape.

1. Two numbers, one portrait

Write a one-DOF linear system as a first-order pair in the state \((x,v)\): $$ \dot{\vb{x}}=\vb{A}\vb{x},\qquad \vb{A}=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-\delta&\tau\end{bmatrix},\qquad \tau=\operatorname{tr}\vb{A},\quad \delta=\det\vb{A}, $$ which is the equation \(\ddot x-\tau\dot x+\delta x=0\). For a mass–spring–damper, \(\tau=-c/m\le0\) and \(\delta=k/m\). Positive \(\tau\) is negative damping (self-excitation); negative \(\delta\) is negative stiffness (a hilltop). The characteristic roots obey $$ \lambda^2-\tau\lambda+\delta=0,\qquad \lambda_1+\lambda_2=\tau,\quad \lambda_1\lambda_2=\delta,\qquad \lambda_{1,2}=\frac{\tau}{2}\pm\sqrt{\frac{\tau^2}{4}-\delta}. $$ So trace is the sum of the roots (twice their common real part when they are complex), which sets growth or decay, and determinant is the product (a negative value means one root on each side of the imaginary axis). The parabola \(\delta=\tau^2/4\) is where the roots collide, and it separates oscillating from non-oscillating behaviour.

  • \(\delta<0\): saddle. One stable and one unstable direction; almost every orbit is eventually thrown out along the unstable eigenvector.
  • \(\delta>0,\ \tau^2<4\delta\): complex roots, spiral. Inward if \(\tau<0\), outward if \(\tau>0\); a closed-orbit center exactly on \(\tau=0\).
  • \(\delta>0,\ \tau^2>4\delta\): real roots of the same sign, node. Stable for \(\tau<0\), unstable for \(\tau>0\); orbits hug the slow eigenvector.
  • \(\delta=0\): one zero root, a whole line of equilibria.

Read-out
What to try
  1. Start at the center and push \(\tau\) slightly negative: closed orbits become inward spirals. Push it positive: outward spirals. The center is a knife edge.
  2. Walk across the parabola at fixed \(\delta\): the spiral unwinds, the oscillation disappears, and the portrait becomes a node whose orbits all bend toward one direction, the slow eigenvector.
  3. Lower \(\delta\) through zero: a stable node becomes a saddle. A stable equilibrium turns into a hilltop. This is the change of curvature of the potential seen in Tutorial 1.
  4. Press Tour to circle through every region continuously.

2. Why this classifies nonlinear equilibria too

For a nonlinear system \(\dot{\vb{x}}=\vb{f}(\vb{x})\), linearize at an equilibrium and read the trace and determinant of the Jacobian \(\vb{J}=\partial\vb{f}/\partial\vb{x}\). Away from the boundaries (hyperbolic cases) the nonlinear portrait looks locally like the linear one. The damped pendulum at the bottom has \(\tau<0,\ \delta>0\): a spiral sink when lightly damped (\(\tau^2<4\delta\)), a stable node when overdamped. At the top \(\delta<0\): a saddle. Centers (\(\tau=0\)) are not robust: a small nonlinearity or damping decides whether the orbit closes or spirals, which is why the damped and undamped pendulum differ qualitatively near the boundary.

3. Check yourself

The roots are \(-1\pm2i\). Where is the point in the trace–determinant plane, and what does the portrait look like?

\(\tau=-2\), \(\delta=1+4=5\). It lies above the parabola (\(\tau^2/4=1<5\)) in the left half: a stable spiral that turns inward with decay rate 1 and angular frequency 2.

Why can a node never spiral?

Real roots mean real eigenvectors: invariant straight lines that orbits cannot cross. Orbits are trapped in the sectors between them and cannot rotate. Complex roots have no real invariant line, so orbits wind around the equilibrium.

What does the trace tell you about the area of a patch of initial conditions?

Liouville's formula: areas in the phase plane change as \(e^{\tau t}\). Watch the comets for \(\tau=0\): a patch keeps its area while it shears and rotates; for \(\tau<0\) it shrinks.
