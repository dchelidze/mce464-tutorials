Roots in the s-Plane

Where the characteristic roots sit decides everything about free decay. Move one slider and watch the roots travel, the envelope change and the regime switch. Then measure the damping back from a noisy decay record.

1. The picture in one line

For \(m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = 0\) the trial solution \(e^{st}\) gives \(s^2 + 2\zeta\omega_n s + \omega_n^2 = 0\). Treat \(s\) as a point in the complex plane:

$$ s_{1,2} = -\zeta\omega_n \pm \omega_n\sqrt{\zeta^2-1} $$
  • Real part \(-\zeta\omega_n\) is the decay rate: how far left of the imaginary axis.
  • Imaginary part \(\pm\omega_d\) is the oscillation frequency: how far above and below the real axis.
  • Distance from the origin is exactly \(\omega_n\) while the roots are complex. Damping slides the roots along a circle.
  • Angle from the negative real axis is \(\theta=\arccos\zeta\). The damping ratio is a direction, not a length.

Read-out
What to notice
  1. \(\zeta=0\): roots sit on the imaginary axis. No decay, pure oscillation.
  2. \(0<\zeta<1\): roots slide left along the circle. The frequency \(\omega_d=\omega_n\sin\theta\) is the height of the root, so it shrinks only slowly at first.
  3. \(\zeta=1\): the roots collide on the negative real axis. This is the critical case, the fastest response without overshoot. It is not the fastest to settle: allowing an overshoot smaller than 2% gives the shortest true 2% settling time, about \(3.60/\omega_n\) at \(\zeta\approx0.78\), against \(5.83/\omega_n\) at \(\zeta=1\) (Section 3).
  4. \(\zeta>1\): the roots split along the real axis with product \(\omega_n^2\). One runs far left (fast, dies quickly), the other creeps back toward the origin (slow). The slow root controls the settling time.

2. Why the circle? The product and sum of roots

The quadratic gives \(s_1 s_2=\omega_n^2\) and \(s_1+s_2=-2\zeta\omega_n\). For complex roots \(s_2=s_1^{*}\), so the product is \(|s_1|^2=\omega_n^2\): the roots stay on a circle of radius \(\omega_n\). Once real, the product still equals \(\omega_n^2\), so the geometric mean of the two roots is \(\omega_n\). The sum, twice the real part, is set by the damping alone.

Design readingFaster settling means pushing the roots left. Less overshoot means pushing them toward the real axis. The choice \(\zeta\approx 0.707\) puts the roots on the \(45^\circ\) lines: the response overshoots by about \(4.3\%\) and settles quickly. The same plane returns in Lecture 19, where the roots become the poles of the transfer function.

3. Settling time: the true value and the envelope estimate

The rule \(t_s\approx 4/|\mathrm{Re}\,s|\) assumes the response is a single exponential \(e^{-\sigma t}\) with unit coefficient, so it reaches 2% when \(e^{-\sigma t}=0.02\), that is at \(\sigma t=\ln 50=3.91\approx4\). The true 2% settling time is the last instant at which \(|x(t)|\ge0.02\). For the release \(x(0)=1,\ \dot x(0)=0\) it is computed here directly from the response.

  • Critical damping. \(x=(1+\omega_n t)e^{-\omega_n t}\). Solving \((1+\tau)e^{-\tau}=0.02\) gives \(t_s=5.834/\omega_n\), not \(4/\omega_n\). The factor \((1+\tau)\) is what the estimate leaves out.
  • Underdamped. \(x=\dfrac{e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\cos(\omega_d t-\psi)\) with \(\tan\psi=\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). The response touches this envelope once every half period, just after each extremum. The true \(t_s\) is set by the last half-cycle that still pokes outside the band.
  • Overdamped. Two exponentials. The slow one dominates late, so \(4/|s_{\rm slow}|\) is close for large \(\zeta\), but its coefficient is not 1.
2% settling time against \(\zeta\), \(x(0)=1,\ \dot x(0)=0\)
Blue: true settling time from \(x(t)\). Orange dashed: \(4/\zeta\) for \(\zeta<1\) and \(4/\bigl(\zeta-\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\bigr)\) for \(\zeta>1\). Red dot: the current \(\zeta\) of Section 1. Curves leave the top of the frame for \(\zeta\lesssim0.17\).
Why a sawtooth

For \(\zeta\lesssim0.8\) the true curve jumps. Each tooth is one extremum of \(x(t)\). As \(\zeta\) grows, that extremum shrinks until it slips inside the \(\pm2\%\) band. At that instant \(t_s\) drops by up to half a damped period, to the moment just after the previous extremum when the response re-enters the band.

The envelope estimate is smooth because it tracks only the decay rate. It ignores where the oscillation is when the envelope crosses 2%, and it drops the factor \(1/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). So it cannot see the teeth.

The smallest true value sits where the first undershoot, \(e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\), equals 2%: \(\zeta^*=0.7797\). Just above \(\zeta^*\) the response enters the band on its first descent and never leaves, \(t_s\approx3.60/\omega_n\). Just below, the undershoot at \(t=\pi/\omega_d\) still counts and \(t_s\approx5.03/\omega_n\). Critical damping is the fastest response without overshoot, not the fastest to settle.

4. Logarithmic decrement from a measured free decay

Lecture 7 turns the free decay around: measure \(x(t)\), read the damping. Successive positive peaks of an underdamped free decay are one damped period \(T_d=2\pi/\omega_d\) apart, and the cosine factor is the same at each, so their ratio is exactly

$$ \frac{x_k}{x_{k+1}}=e^{\zeta\omega_n T_d}, \qquad \delta=\ln\frac{x_k}{x_{k+1}}=\frac{2\pi\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}=\frac1n\ln\frac{x_0}{x_n}, \qquad \zeta=\frac{\delta}{\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}} $$

For small damping \(\zeta\approx\delta/(2\pi)\). That approximation overestimates \(\zeta\) by the factor \(1/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\). Here the release from \(x(0)=1\) at rest is sampled every \(\Delta t=0.2/\omega_n\) (about 31 samples per period) and Gaussian noise of standard deviation \(\sigma\) is added. A sample counts as a peak if it is positive and the largest within \(\pm0.7\) of a nominal period \(2\pi/\omega_n\). Peaks are found from the noisy samples only, as you would from a measured record.

Limits of the method (Lecture 7)
  • Noise. Picking the largest sample near a peak favours positive noise, so small peaks read high and \(\delta\) comes out low. Set \(\kappa=0\) and watch the least-squares estimate fall. Use peaks well above the noise floor, and prefer a fit over many strong peaks to two weak ones.
  • Sampling. A sampled peak misses the true peak by up to \(1-\cos(\omega_d\Delta t/2)\approx0.5\%\) here. Over \(n\) periods this error is divided by \(n\).
  • Nonviscous damping. The formula assumes \(c\dot x\). Coulomb friction makes the peaks fall linearly, not geometrically, and amplitude-dependent damping bends the line; then \(\delta\) depends on which peaks you choose. A curved \(\ln x_k\) plot is the warning sign.
  • Beating. Two modes with close frequencies modulate the peaks. \(\ln x_k\) then oscillates about a line and a two-peak estimate can be badly wrong. Isolate one mode first (filtering, or excitation of one mode).

5. The phase-plane spiral

Plot the same free decay as a trajectory in the plane \((x,\ \dot x/\omega_n)\). Because the system is linear and the motion repeats its shape every damped period, the state one period later is the present state scaled down: $$ \big(x,\dot x\big)(t+T_d)=e^{-\zeta\omega_nT_d}\,\big(x,\dot x\big)(t)=e^{-\delta}\big(x,\dot x\big)(t). $$ So every ray from the origin cuts the spiral in a geometric sequence with ratio \(e^{\delta}\), whatever its angle. The peaks of \(x(t)\) are the crossings of the ray along the positive \(x\)-axis (\(\dot x=0\), \(x>0\)), which is why the peak ratio is exact even though the peaks do not lie on the envelope. Drag in the plane to turn the ray. In the coordinates \(\big(x,\ (\dot x+\zeta\omega_nx)/\omega_d\big)\) the spiral is an exact logarithmic spiral: its radius is the envelope \(e^{-\zeta\omega_nt}\) itself. The damping ratio is the \(\zeta\) of section 4.

6. Check yourself

Two systems have roots \(-1\pm 3i\) and \(-3\pm 3i\). Which decays faster, and which oscillates faster?

The second decays faster (real part \(-3\) versus \(-1\)). Both have \(\omega_d=3\), so they oscillate at the same frequency. Their natural frequencies differ: \(\sqrt{10}\) and \(\sqrt{18}\).

At \(\zeta=1\) the roots form a double root. Why does the response contain a factor \(t\)?

Two exponentials \(e^{s_1t}\) and \(e^{s_2t}\) merge into one, so a second independent solution is needed. The limit \[ \lim_{s_1\to s_2}\frac{e^{s_1t}-e^{s_2t}}{s_1-s_2}=t\,e^{st}. \]

Heavily overdamped: why does more damping make the system slower?

The slow root \(-\omega_n\bigl(\zeta-\sqrt{\zeta^2-1}\bigr)\) moves back toward the origin as \(\zeta\) grows, approaching \(-\omega_n/(2\zeta)\). The damper holds the mass back.

A free decay has peaks \(1.00\) and \(0.20\) five periods apart. Find \(\zeta\) exactly and with \(\zeta\approx\delta/(2\pi)\).

\(\delta=\tfrac15\ln 5=0.3219\). Exactly, \(\zeta=\delta/\sqrt{4\pi^2+\delta^2}=0.05116\). The approximation gives \(0.05123\), 0.13% high. At this damping the approximation is harmless; the noise in the peaks matters far more.
