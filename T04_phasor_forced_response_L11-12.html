Phasors and the Forced Response

Magnitude and phase are one geometric fact: three force vectors that must close into a polygon. Sweep the frequency ratio and watch the polygon, the rotating phasors and the response curves move together.

1. Forces as vectors

Drive \(m\ddot x + c\dot x + kx = F_0\cos\Omega t\). In steady state every term is a harmonic at \(\Omega\), so each one is a rotating vector (a phasor). With \(x=X\cos(\Omega t-\phi)\):

  • Spring force \(kX\), in phase with the displacement.
  • Damper force \(c\Omega X\), \(90^\circ\) ahead of the displacement (velocity leads).
  • Inertia force \(-m\Omega^2X\), \(180^\circ\) from the displacement.

Newton's law says their sum is the applied force \(F_0\). Laid head to tail they close onto the red vector. Everything in Lectures 11–12 (the magnitude formula, the phase formula, resonance) can be read off this picture.

The three response forces add to \(F_0\) (red, length 1). The angle \(\phi\) between \(F_0\) and the spring vector is the phase lag.

Table of readings
Three regimes, read from the polygon
  1. Low frequency, \(r\ll1\). The spring vector is almost the whole force: \(D\approx1\), \(\phi\approx0\). The system is stiffness-controlled and follows the load quasi-statically.
  2. Resonance, \(r=1\). Spring and inertia vectors are equal and opposite and cancel. The damper alone balances \(F_0\): \(c\Omega X=F_0\), so \(D=1/(2\zeta)\) and \(\phi=90^\circ\).
  3. High frequency, \(r\gg1\). The inertia vector dominates and points opposite to \(x\): \(\phi\to180^\circ\), \(D\approx1/r^2\). The mass cannot follow.

2. Magnitude, phase and the mobility circle

Closing the polygon with Pythagoras and a tangent gives the lecture formulas:

$$ D(r)=\frac{kX}{F_0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}},\qquad \phi=\operatorname{atan2}\bigl(2\zeta r,\;1-r^2\bigr) $$

The third panel below plots the mobility (velocity per unit force) in the complex plane. It traces an exact circle of diameter \(1/(2\zeta)\) through the origin. With \(Y=ir/(1-r^2+2i\zeta r)\), $$ \frac1Y = 2\zeta - i\,\frac{1-r^2}{r}, $$ a vertical straight line \(\operatorname{Re}(1/Y)=2\zeta\), and inversion maps a line not through the origin to a circle through it. The half-power points sit where \(\operatorname{Re}Y=\pm\operatorname{Im}Y\) (\(45^\circ\) on the circle).

Bandwidth, geometricallyThe half-power points are where the response phasor has turned \(45^\circ\) either side of the resonance diameter of the circle. When \(\zeta\) is small, very little change in \(r\) covers that arc, which is why a sharp peak identifies damping so well: \(\Delta r=2\zeta\).

3. Check yourself

At \(r=1\) the spring force is large but it does not appear in the force balance. Why?

The inertia force equals it in magnitude and points the opposite way, so the two cancel. The mass and spring are exchanging energy with each other, and the external force only has to pay for the damper.

Why does \(\phi\) run from \(0\) to \(180^\circ\) and not further?

The damper vector is always \(90^\circ\) ahead of the displacement, and the work it takes from the load must be positive, which requires \(\sin\phi>0\) (\(2\zeta r>0\)). Hence \(0<\phi<180^\circ\).

If \(\zeta\) doubles, what happens to the peak and to the bandwidth?

The peak \(D\) halves (about \(1/(2\zeta)\)) and the half-power bandwidth doubles (\(\Delta r=2\zeta\)); their product stays fixed.
