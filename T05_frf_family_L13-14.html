One Curve, Many Machines

Base excitation, transmissibility, rotating unbalance, seismometers and accelerometers look like separate chapters. Geometrically they are one vector triangle and three rescalings of a single magnification curve.

1. Base motion as a triangle

A base moves as \(y=Y\cos\Omega t\) and the mass responds with absolute motion \(x\). Write \(z=x-y\) for the relative displacement (spring stretch). The relative equation is a forced oscillator driven by the base acceleration: $$ m\ddot z+c\dot z+kz=-m\ddot y=m\Omega^2Y\cos\Omega t. $$ Comparing with Tutorial 4, the phasors obey $$ \frac{Z}{Y}=r^2D(r)\,e^{-i\phi},\qquad D=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2}},\qquad X=Y+Z. $$ So the absolute motion is the sum of two vectors: the unit base vector and a relative vector of length \(r^2D\), turned by the usual phase lag \(\phi\). The length of the sum is the transmissibility $$ T_d(r)=\left|\frac{X}{Y}\right|=\sqrt{1+(2\zeta r)^2}\;D(r). $$

  • \(r\ll1\): \(Z\to0\), so \(X\to Y\). The mass rides rigidly with the base.
  • \(r=1\): \(Z\) points straight down with length \(1/(2\zeta)\). The amplitude is \(\sqrt{1+1/(4\zeta^2)}\).
  • \(r\gg1\): \(Z\to-Y\) (length 1, angle \(180^\circ\)), so \(X\to0\). The mass stands still in space and the spring stretch simply copies the base motion. This is the seismometer principle.

The isolation threshold

Set \(T_d=1\): \((2\zeta r)^2+1=(1-r^2)^2+(2\zeta r)^2\), so \(r^2(r^2-2)=0\) and \(r=\sqrt2\). Below \(\sqrt2\) the suspension amplifies; above it, it isolates. Damping does not move the threshold. Above it, more damping hurts, because the damper passes high-frequency motion straight through: the term \(\sqrt{1+(2\zeta r)^2}\) grows with \(\zeta\).

For the force transmitted to the support by a machine with force \(F_0\), the same function applies, \(F_T/F_0=T_d(r)\). For base motion, \(F_T/(kY)=r^2T_d\).

3. Check yourself

Why does the relative motion \(Z\) follow the usual single-DOF magnification, even though the base moves?

Write \(x=y+z\). The spring and damper see only \(z\) and \(\dot z\), and the mass sees \(\ddot x=\ddot y+\ddot z\). Moving \(m\ddot y\) to the right-hand side turns the base motion into an effective force \(-m\ddot y\), which grows as \(\Omega^2\), hence the factor \(r^2\).

An engine is mounted with \(\omega_n\) = 10 Hz and \(\zeta\) = 0.1. At what engine speed does the isolator begin to help?

At \(r=\sqrt2\), i.e. \(f=14.1\) Hz (about 850 rpm). Below it the mount amplifies, with a peak of about \(\sqrt{1+(0.2)^2}/(0.2)\approx5.1\) at resonance (10 Hz).

Why does adding damping improve an accelerometer but worsen isolation at high frequency?

For the accelerometer the goal is a flat \(D\) at low \(r\), and the resonance bump is what damping suppresses. For isolation we want \(T_d\) small at \(r\gg1\); there \(T_d\approx2\zeta r\,D\approx 2\zeta/r\) grows with \(\zeta\).
