Dissipation Loops and the Nonlinear FRF

Every damping model is a closed loop in the force–displacement plane, and the loop area is the energy lost per cycle. Its dependence on amplitude and frequency decides how the frequency response bends, jumps and depends on the sweep direction.

1. The energy lost per cycle is an area

For steady motion \(x=X\cos\Omega t\), the damping force \(F_d\) traces a closed curve against \(x\). The work it removes per cycle is the enclosed area, $$ \Delta W=\oint F_d\,\mathrm dx. $$ Four models, scaled here to lose the same energy \(W_0\) per cycle at \(X=1\) and \(r=\Omega/\omega_n=1\), so that their different scaling is what you see:

2. From loss to the frequency response

Add a Duffing spring, \(\ddot x+F_d+x+\varepsilon x^3=f\cos\Omega t\), with \(\omega_n=1\). Keep only the fundamental harmonic (harmonic balance). Each loop then acts as an amplitude-dependent equivalent damping \(G(X,r)\): the fundamental-harmonic damping force is in phase with the velocity (in quadrature with the displacement), and \(G\) is its amplitude per unit displacement amplitude: $$ \Bigl[\bigl(1-r^2+\tfrac34\varepsilon X^2\bigr)^2+G(X,r)^2\Bigr]X^2=f^2,\qquad G=\frac{W_0}{\pi}\times\bigl\{\,r,\;1,\;1/X,\;r^2X\,\bigr\}. $$ The stiffness term \(\tfrac34\varepsilon X^2\) bends the resonance (the backbone \(r^2=1+\tfrac34\varepsilon X^2\)), and \(G\) sets its height. For each \(r\) the equation can have one or three amplitudes. Where there are three, the middle branch is unstable (it is the one the sweeps never land on), and the response depends on which way you sweep: it follows the nearest stable branch until that branch ends, then jumps.

What to notice
  1. With \(\varepsilon=0\) the four models give different peak heights and shapes: Coulomb loss matters most at small amplitude, quadratic drag matters most at large amplitude, and for quadratic drag the small-amplitude limit is lossless.
  2. Raise \(\varepsilon>0\): the peak leans right (hardening). Once the lean exceeds the width set by \(G\), a three-valued region appears and the sweeps separate.
  3. Lower \(f\) or raise \(W_0\): damping beats the lean and the hysteresis closes. Negative \(\varepsilon\) (softening) leans left and reverses which sweep jumps up.
  4. Coulomb: friction removes \(4F_cX\) per cycle, the drive at most \(\pi fX\). Both grow like \(X\), so harmonic balance gives motion only if \(f>W_0/\pi\); below that the mass sticks. Above it, with \(\varepsilon=0\), \(X=\sqrt{f^2-(W_0/\pi)^2}\,/\,|1-r^2|\), which is unbounded at \(r=1\): friction cannot limit a resonance. Try \(f=0.05\), then \(f=0.1\) with \(\varepsilon=0\).

3. Free decay under the damping models

Release the oscillator \(\ddot x+x+F_d=0\) from \(x_0=1,\ v_0=0\) and integrate with RK4 (step \(h=0.002\)). The three models use the forces of Section 1, so they lose the same energy \(W_0\) per cycle at \(X=1\): \(c=W_0/\pi\), \(F_c=W_0/4\), \(c_2=3W_0/8\). Averaging the energy loss over one cycle, \(\mathrm d(\tfrac12X^2)/\mathrm dt=-\Delta W(X)/2\pi\), gives the envelopes, all with the same initial slope \(a=W_0/2\pi\): $$ \text{viscous: } X=e^{-at},\qquad \text{Coulomb: } X=1-at,\qquad \text{quadratic: } X=\frac{1}{1+at}. $$ Coulomb damping loses a fixed \(4F_c/k\) of amplitude per cycle, so the envelope is a straight line. Each half-cycle is undamped motion about a shifted centre, \(x=+F_c/k\) while moving left and \(x=-F_c/k\) while moving right, so in the phase plane it is an arc of a circle centred at \(\pm F_c/k\). The mass stops for good at the first turning point inside the dead zone \(|x|\le F_c/k\), where the spring cannot overcome friction: a finite stopping time, a whole number of half-periods. Viscous and quadratic decays never stop in finite time; quadratic drag fades fast at large amplitude and slowly at small amplitude.

Structural (hysteretic) damping is not shown. Its force \(\eta k\,\dot x/\Omega\) is defined only for harmonic motion at a given \(\Omega\); a frequency-independent loss has no causal time-domain model, so it has no free decay to integrate.

4. Check yourself

Why can a Coulomb-damped system not be described by a single damping ratio?

Its equivalent damping \(G=4\mu N/(\pi X)\) depends on amplitude: small motion is damped heavily, large motion lightly, so the "damping ratio" is a function of the response itself.

The structural loop has the same area at every frequency. How does the FRF differ from the viscous one?

Viscous loss grows with \(r\), so it is large above resonance and small below. Structural loss is flat, so the force in quadrature with the displacement does not vanish at low \(r\) and the phase leaves zero immediately.

Why does the sweep-up curve jump down while the sweep-down curve jumps up for a hardening spring?

The branches are connected by an unstable middle branch and bounded by two turning points. Sweeping up, the upper branch ends at the right turning point and the amplitude drops to the lower branch. Sweeping down, the lower branch ends at the left turning point and the amplitude rises to the upper branch.
