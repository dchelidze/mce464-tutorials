Energy Surfaces and the Mass-Weighted Transform

Kinetic and potential energy are two paraboloids over the same plane. In physical coordinates their axes disagree, which is what coupling means. Stretch the plane by \(\vb{M}^{1/2}\), then rotate it, and both surfaces align at once: those are the modal coordinates.

1. Two bowls over one plane

For a two-DOF system, \(T=\tfrac12\dot{\vb{x}}^{\T}\vb{M}\dot{\vb{x}}\) and \(V=\tfrac12\vb{x}^{\T}\vb{K}\vb{x}\) are positive-definite quadratic forms. Each is a paraboloid whose level curves are ellipses. The potential bowl (left, below) sits over the displacement plane; the kinetic bowl (right) sits over the velocity plane. In the physical coordinates \((x_1,x_2)\) the two bowls have different principal axes, and neither is aligned with the coordinate axes when the masses are coupled. A free vibration is a ball rolling on the \(V\) surface while a partner ball rides the \(T\) surface; the heights trade places while \(T+V\) stays fixed.

2. Making both bowls round and aligned

Two linear changes of axes do the job. First the mass-weighted coordinates $$ \vb{y}=\vb{M}^{1/2}\vb{x}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T=\tfrac12\dot{\vb{y}}^{\T}\dot{\vb{y}},\qquad V=\tfrac12\,\vb{y}^{\T}\tilde{\vb{K}}\,\vb{y},\qquad \tilde{\vb{K}}=\vb{M}^{-1/2}\vb{K}\vb{M}^{-1/2}. $$ The kinetic bowl is now a circular paraboloid (any rotation leaves it unchanged), and \(\tilde{\vb{K}}\) is symmetric, so the equations \(\ddot{\vb{y}}+\tilde{\vb{K}}\vb{y}=\vb{0}\) pose a standard eigenproblem with orthonormal eigenvectors \(\vb{\psi}_r\). Second, rotate onto them: $$ \vb{q}=\vb{\Psi}^{\T}\vb{y}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T=\tfrac12\sum_r\dot q_r^2,\qquad V=\tfrac12\sum_r\omega_r^2q_r^2 . $$ Both bowls are now axis-aligned, and \(q_r\) are the modal coordinates of Tutorial 11, because \(\vb{\phi}_r=\vb{M}^{-1/2}\vb{\psi}_r\) and $$ \vb{q}=\vb{\Psi}^{\T}\vb{M}^{1/2}\vb{x}=\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{M}\,\vb{x}. $$ In two dimensions the second step is a pure rotation by the angle \(-\alpha\) of the first eigenvector \(\vb{\psi}_1\).

What to watch
  1. 0 → 1. The ink ellipse becomes the dotted unit circle and the kinetic mesh becomes round. The potential bowl is still tilted: \(\tilde{\vb{K}}\) is not diagonal.
  2. 1 → 2. A pure rotation. Nothing is stretched, so the kinetic bowl stays round, while the potential bowl turns until its axes are the coordinate axes. The mode lines become the \(q_1\) and \(q_2\) axes.
  3. For a single mode (set \(\alpha_0\) to 0 or 90°) the ball runs along a principal parabola of the bowl: a one-DOF oscillator hidden inside the plane.
  4. Set \(k_2=0\): the masses decouple. The rotation angle \(\alpha\) is then 0 or 90°, and only the scaling stage matters.
  5. Make \(m_1=m_2\): the scaling stage is a uniform zoom, so the shape of the potential bowl is unchanged by it.

3. Check yourself

Why is the second step a rotation and not another stretch?

After the first step the kinetic metric is the identity. Only transformations that preserve \(\dot{\vb{y}}^{\T}\dot{\vb{y}}\) keep it that way: rotations and reflections. The potential matrix \(\tilde{\vb{K}}\) is symmetric, so by the spectral theorem such an orthogonal change of axes diagonalizes it.

Why are mass-normalized modes \(\vb{\phi}_r=\vb{M}^{-1/2}\vb{\psi}_r\) generally not perpendicular in the physical plane?

The map \(\vb{M}^{-1/2}\) stretches the plane by different amounts along different axes, so perpendicular \(\vb{\psi}_r\) are sent to non-perpendicular \(\vb{\phi}_r\). They are perpendicular in the \(\vb{M}\)-weighted inner product, \(\vb{\phi}_i^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{\phi}_j=\delta_{ij}\), which is the ordinary dot product in \(\vb{y}\).

How does a Cholesky factor \(\vb{M}=\vb{L}\vb{L}^{\T}\) compare with \(\vb{M}^{1/2}\)?

Any \(\vb{S}\) with \(\vb{S}^{\T}\vb{S}=\vb{M}\) makes the kinetic bowl round. They differ by a rotation, so the eigenvectors of the transformed stiffness are rotated accordingly; the frequencies and the \(\vb{q}\) coordinates (up to sign) are identical. \(\vb{M}^{1/2}\) is the symmetric choice and is convenient when \(\vb{M}\) is diagonal.
