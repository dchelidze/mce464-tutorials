Energy Surfaces and the Mass-Weighted Transform

Kinetic and potential energy are two paraboloids over the same plane. In physical coordinates their axes disagree, which is what coupling means. It also covers systems with inertial coupling, where \(\vb{M}\) itself is not diagonal. Stretch the plane by \(\vb{M}^{1/2}\), then rotate it, and both surfaces align at once: those are the modal coordinates.

1. Two bowls over one plane

In Lecture 20 the mass matrix comes from the kinetic energy written in generalized coordinates, \(T=\tfrac12\dot{\vb{q}}^{\T}\vb{M}\dot{\vb{q}}\), and \(\vb{K}\) from the potential energy, \(V=\tfrac12\vb{q}^{\T}\vb{K}\vb{q}\). Both are positive-definite quadratic forms. Each is a paraboloid whose level curves are ellipses. The potential bowl (left, below) sits over the displacement plane; the kinetic bowl (right) sits over the velocity plane. A free vibration is a ball rolling on the \(V\) surface while a partner ball rides the \(T\) surface; the heights trade places while \(T+V\) stays fixed.

Pick a system below. For the two-mass chain \(\vb{M}\) is diagonal, so the kinetic bowl is already aligned with the axes and only its widths differ. For the double pendulum and the cart with a pendulum, the generalized coordinates share kinetic energy through a cross term \(M_{12}\neq 0\). Then the kinetic bowl is tilted in the \(\vb{q}\) plane, just like the potential bowl can be.

Two-mass chainCoordinates \(q_1,q_2\) (displacements). \(T=\tfrac12(m_1\dot q_1^2+m_2\dot q_2^2)\), so \(\vb{M}=\mathrm{diag}(m_1,m_2)\). \(V=\tfrac12[k_1q_1^2+k_2(q_2-q_1)^2+k_3q_2^2]\), so \(\vb{K}=\begin{bmatrix}k_1+k_2&-k_2\\-k_2&k_2+k_3\end{bmatrix}\). Coupling is in \(\vb{K}\) only.
Double pendulum, small anglesCoordinates \(\theta_1,\theta_2\). With \(\vb{r}_1=l_1(\sin\theta_1,-\cos\theta_1)\) and \(\vb{r}_2=\vb{r}_1+l_2(\sin\theta_2,-\cos\theta_2)\): $$T=\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)l_1^2\dot\theta_1^2+m_2l_1l_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2+\tfrac12m_2l_2^2\dot\theta_2^2,\qquad V=-(m_1+m_2)g\,l_1\cos\theta_1-m_2g\,l_2\cos\theta_2 .$$ Set \(\cos\to1\) in \(T\) and \(\cos\theta\to1-\theta^2/2\) in \(V\): $$\vb{M}=\begin{bmatrix}(m_1+m_2)l_1^2&m_2l_1l_2\\m_2l_1l_2&m_2l_2^2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \vb{K}=\begin{bmatrix}(m_1+m_2)g\,l_1&0\\0&m_2g\,l_2\end{bmatrix}.$$ Coupling is in \(\vb{M}\) only. The potential bowl is already aligned with the axes; the kinetic bowl is not.
Cart carrying a pendulum, small angleCoordinates \(\vb{q}=(x,\ \theta)\): cart displacement \(x\) (a length) and pendulum angle \(\theta\). The cart (\(m_1\)) is held by a spring \(k\); the bob (\(m_2\)) hangs on a rod of length \(l\). The bob velocity is \((\dot x+l\dot\theta\cos\theta,\ l\dot\theta\sin\theta)\), so $$T=\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)\dot x^2+m_2l\cos\theta\,\dot x\dot\theta+\tfrac12m_2l^2\dot\theta^2,\qquad V=\tfrac12kx^2-m_2g\,l\cos\theta,$$ $$\vb{M}=\begin{bmatrix}m_1+m_2&m_2l\\m_2l&m_2l^2\end{bmatrix},\qquad \vb{K}=\begin{bmatrix}k&0\\0&m_2g\,l\end{bmatrix}.$$ The two coordinates have different units, which is fine: the quadratic forms \(T\) and \(V\) are what matter. Coupling is in \(\vb{M}\) only.

2. Making both bowls round and aligned

Two linear changes of axes do the job. First the mass-weighted coordinates $$ \tilde{\vb{q}}=\vb{M}^{1/2}\vb{q}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T=\tfrac12\dot{\tilde{\vb{q}}}^{\T}\dot{\tilde{\vb{q}}},\qquad V=\tfrac12\,\tilde{\vb{q}}^{\T}\tilde{\vb{K}}\,\tilde{\vb{q}},\qquad \tilde{\vb{K}}=\vb{M}^{-1/2}\vb{K}\vb{M}^{-1/2}. $$ Here \(\vb{M}^{1/2}\) is the symmetric square root. Write \(\vb{M}=\vb{V}\,\mathrm{diag}(\mu_i)\,\vb{V}^{\T}\) with orthonormal \(\vb{V}\); then \(\vb{M}^{\tau/2}=\vb{V}\,\mathrm{diag}(\mu_i^{\tau/2})\,\vb{V}^{\T}\). Stage 1 of the page morphs the plane by \(\vb{M}^{\tau/2}\) for \(\tau\) from 0 to 1. This is a stretch by \(\mu_i^{\tau/2}\) along the principal axes of \(\vb{M}\). When \(\vb{M}\) is diagonal those axes are the coordinate axes and the stage is plain axis scaling. When \(\vb{M}\) has a cross term, the principal axes are tilted, so the stage stretches along tilted directions and reshapes the tilted kinetic ellipse into a circle. The kinetic bowl still becomes round at \(\tau=1\).

The kinetic bowl is now a circular paraboloid (any rotation leaves it unchanged), and \(\tilde{\vb{K}}\) is symmetric, so the equations \(\ddot{\tilde{\vb{q}}}+\tilde{\vb{K}}\tilde{\vb{q}}=\vb{0}\) pose a standard eigenproblem with orthonormal eigenvectors \(\vb{\psi}_r\). Second, rotate onto them: $$ \vb{\eta}=\vb{\Psi}^{\T}\tilde{\vb{q}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T=\tfrac12\sum_r\dot {\eta}_r^2,\qquad V=\tfrac12\sum_r\omega_r^2{\eta}_r^2 . $$ Both bowls are now axis-aligned, and \({\eta}_r\) are the modal coordinates of Tutorial 11, because \(\vb{\phi}_r=\vb{M}^{-1/2}\vb{\psi}_r\) and $$ \vb{\eta}=\vb{\Psi}^{\T}\vb{M}^{1/2}\vb{q}=\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{M}\,\vb{q}. $$ In two dimensions the second step is a pure rotation by the angle \(-\alpha\) of the first eigenvector \(\vb{\psi}_1\). (The sign of \(\vb{\psi}_2\) is chosen as \(\vb{\psi}_1\) turned by \(+90^\circ\), so the step is a proper rotation; this fixes the sign of \(\vb{\phi}_2\) and \({\eta}_2\) and nothing else.) The page checks the identity \(\vb{\Psi}^{\T}\vb{M}^{1/2}=\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{M}\) numerically in the table below.

What to watch
  1. 0 → 1. The ink ellipse \(\vb{q}^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{q}=1\) becomes the dotted unit circle and the kinetic mesh becomes round. For the pendulum systems that ellipse starts tilted, so the stretch runs along the tilted principal axes of \(\vb{M}\), not along \(q_1\) and \(q_2\). The potential bowl is still tilted: \(\tilde{\vb{K}}\) is not diagonal.
  2. 1 → 2. A pure rotation. Nothing is stretched, so the kinetic bowl stays round, while the potential bowl turns until its axes are the coordinate axes. The mode lines become the \({\eta}_1\) and \({\eta}_2\) axes.
  3. For a single mode (set \(\alpha_0\) to 0 or 90°) the ball runs along a principal parabola of the bowl: a one-DOF oscillator hidden inside the plane.
  4. Chain, \(k_2=0\): the masses decouple. The rotation angle \(\alpha\) is then 0 or 90°, and only the scaling stage matters.
  5. Chain, \(m_1=m_2\): the scaling stage is a uniform zoom, so the shape of the potential bowl is unchanged by it. For the pendulum systems \(\vb{M}\) is never a multiple of \(\vb{I}\) here, so stage 1 always reshapes the plane.
  6. Double pendulum: \(\vb{K}\) is diagonal, so the potential bowl starts aligned. The coupling lives entirely in the tilted kinetic bowl, and stage 1 turns it into a tilted potential bowl.

3. Projecting an initial condition onto the modes

For mass-normalized modes, initial conditions in physical coordinates go to modal coordinates through \(\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{M}\), not through \(\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\) alone: $$ \vb{\eta}(0)=\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{M}\,\vb{q}(0),\qquad \dot{\vb{\eta}}(0)=\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{M}\,\dot{\vb{q}}(0). $$ The table treats the ball's present state as an initial condition and applies this live. Each \({\eta}_r\) then evolves as its own oscillator, \(\ddot {\eta}_r+\omega_r^2{\eta}_r=0\), and the energies computed from \(\vb{\eta}\) match those computed from \(\vb{q}\).

4. Check yourself

Why is the second step a rotation and not another stretch?

After the first step the kinetic metric is the identity. Only transformations that preserve \(\dot{\tilde{\vb{q}}}^{\T}\dot{\tilde{\vb{q}}}\) keep it that way: rotations and reflections. The potential matrix \(\tilde{\vb{K}}\) is symmetric, so by the spectral theorem such an orthogonal change of axes diagonalizes it.

Why are mass-normalized modes \(\vb{\phi}_r=\vb{M}^{-1/2}\vb{\psi}_r\) generally not perpendicular in the physical plane?

The map \(\vb{M}^{-1/2}\) stretches the plane by different amounts along different axes, so perpendicular \(\vb{\psi}_r\) are sent to non-perpendicular \(\vb{\phi}_r\). They are perpendicular in the \(\vb{M}\)-weighted inner product, \(\vb{\phi}_i^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{\phi}_j=\delta_{ij}\), which is the ordinary dot product in \(\tilde{\vb{q}}\). They are also \(\vb{K}\)-orthogonal, \(\vb{\phi}_i^{\T}\vb{K}\vb{\phi}_j=\omega_i^2\delta_{ij}\).

Why does the double pendulum have a tilted kinetic ellipse but a diagonal \(\vb{K}\)?

Both bobs move when \(\theta_1\) changes, so the velocity of bob 2 contains \(l_1\dot\theta_1\) and \(l_2\dot\theta_2\) together, and squaring it produces the cross term \(m_2l_1l_2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\). The gravitational potential, in contrast, is a sum of one term per angle. Which matrix carries the coupling depends on the coordinates, not only on the physics: choose absolute angles and the coupling sits in \(\vb{M}\); choose the relative angle \(\theta_2-\theta_1\) and it moves into \(\vb{K}\) as well.

How does a Cholesky factor \(\vb{M}=\vb{L}\vb{L}^{\T}\) compare with \(\vb{M}^{1/2}\)?

Any \(\vb{S}\) with \(\vb{S}^{\T}\vb{S}=\vb{M}\) makes the kinetic bowl round. They differ by a rotation, so the eigenvectors of the transformed stiffness are rotated accordingly; the frequencies and the \(\vb{\eta}\) coordinates (up to sign) are identical. \(\vb{M}^{1/2}\) is the symmetric choice. It is unique and involves no tilt of its own, but for a non-diagonal \(\vb{M}\) it needs the eigendecomposition of \(\vb{M}\), which a Cholesky factor does not.
