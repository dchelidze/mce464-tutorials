Modal Coordinates: Rotating Into Decoupled Axes

The modal transformation is a change of axes. In the new axes the inertia ellipse becomes a circle and the stiffness ellipse lines up with the axes, so the equations decouple into independent oscillators.

1. One point, two coordinate systems

Collect the mass-normalized mode shapes in \(\vb{\Phi}=[\vb{\phi}_1\ \vb{\phi}_2]\) and write \(\vb{x}=\vb{\Phi}\vb{q}\). Then $$ \vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{\Phi}=\vb{I},\qquad \vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{K}\vb{\Phi}=\operatorname{diag}(\omega_1^2,\omega_2^2). $$ Geometrically, \(\vb{x}=q_1\vb{\phi}_1+q_2\vb{\phi}_2\) expands the same point along the two mode directions (the parallelogram rule, in blue and orange below). Because the axes are skew, the components are not the ordinary perpendicular projections. They are the \(\vb{M}\)-weighted ones: $$ q_r=\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{M}\,\vb{x}. $$ In the \(\vb{q}\)-plane the inertia ellipse \(\vb{x}^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{x}=q_1^2+q_2^2=1\) is a unit circle, and the stiffness ellipse \(\vb{x}^{\T}\vb{K}\vb{x}=\omega_1^2q_1^2+\omega_2^2q_2^2=1\) has its axes on the coordinate axes with semi-axes \(1/\omega_r\).

What decoupling buys you

In modal coordinates \(\vb{M}\ddot{\vb{x}}+\vb{K}\vb{x}=\vb{f}\) becomes two independent scalar equations $$ \ddot q_r+\omega_r^2q_r=\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{f}\quad(r=1,2). $$ Energy splits cleanly: \(\tfrac12\vb{x}^{\T}\vb{K}\vb{x}=\tfrac12\sum_r\omega_r^2q_r^2\), with no cross terms. Each mode can be solved with the single-DOF tools of Lectures 5–19 and the answers added back through \(\vb{x}=\vb{\Phi}\vb{q}\).

2. Damping and forced response by modes

Take Rayleigh damping, \(\vb{C}=\alpha\vb{M}+\beta\vb{K}\). Then \(\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{C}\vb{\Phi}=\alpha\vb{I}+\beta\operatorname{diag}(\omega_r^2)\) is diagonal, and each mode has $$ \zeta_r=\frac{\alpha+\beta\omega_r^2}{2\omega_r}. $$ The receptance of mass \(i\) to a force on mass \(j\) is a sum of one-DOF terms, $$ H_{ij}(\Omega)=\sum_{r}\frac{\phi_{ir}\,\phi_{jr}}{\omega_r^2-\Omega^2+2i\zeta_r\omega_r\Omega}. $$ Switch a mode off to see what truncation costs. A dropped mode leaves a static (stiffness-like) remainder \(\phi_{ir}\phi_{jr}/\omega_r^2\) at low frequency: the "static correction". Add a damper \(c_g\) from mass 2 to ground to break proportionality and see where the diagonal modal model starts to fail (Lecture 22, nonproportional damping).

3. Check yourself

Why are the modal components not the perpendicular projections of \(\vb{x}\) on the mode lines?

The modes are orthogonal with respect to \(\vb{M}\), not with respect to the identity. The correct projection weights the dot product by \(\vb{M}\): \(q_r=\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{x}\). The two agree only when \(\vb{M}\propto\vb{I}\) and the modes happen to be perpendicular.

When \(\Omega\to0\), how do the modal contributions add up to the static deflection?

Each mode contributes \(\phi_{ir}\phi_{jr}/\omega_r^2\), and the sum is \((\vb{K}^{-1})_{ij}\). Low modes with small \(\omega_r\) dominate, but the high modes still contribute a smooth static piece.

What is "nonproportional" damping geometrically?

The damping form \(\vb{x}^{\T}\vb{C}\vb{x}\) is a third ellipse whose axes do not line up with the common axes of the \(\vb{M}\) and \(\vb{K}\) ellipses. The change of axes that diagonalizes \(\vb{M}\) and \(\vb{K}\) leaves \(\vb{C}\) with off-diagonal terms, so the modes talk to each other.
