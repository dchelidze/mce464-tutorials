Modal Coordinates: Rotating Into Decoupled Axes

The modal transformation is a change of axes. In the new axes the inertia ellipse becomes a circle and the stiffness ellipse lines up with the axes, so the equations decouple into independent oscillators. Three further views follow: where a force goes (modal forces), how the modal terms add in the complex plane, and the free response from arbitrary initial conditions.

1. One point, two coordinate systems

Collect the mass-normalized mode shapes in \(\vb{\Phi}=[\vb{\phi}_1\ \vb{\phi}_2]\) and write \(\vb{q}=\vb{\Phi}\vb{\eta}\). Then $$ \vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{\Phi}=\vb{I},\qquad \vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{K}\vb{\Phi}=\operatorname{diag}(\omega_1^2,\omega_2^2). $$ Geometrically, \(\vb{q}={\eta}_1\vb{\phi}_1+{\eta}_2\vb{\phi}_2\) expands the same point along the two mode directions (the parallelogram rule, in blue and orange below). Because the axes are skew, the components are not the ordinary perpendicular projections. They are the \(\vb{M}\)-weighted ones: $$ {\eta}_r=\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{M}\,\vb{q}. $$ In the \(\vb{\eta}\)-plane the inertia ellipse \(\vb{q}^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{q}={\eta}_1^2+{\eta}_2^2=1\) is a unit circle, and the stiffness ellipse \(\vb{q}^{\T}\vb{K}\vb{q}=\omega_1^2{\eta}_1^2+\omega_2^2{\eta}_2^2=1\) has its axes on the coordinate axes with semi-axes \(1/\omega_r\).

What decoupling buys you

In modal coordinates \(\vb{M}\ddot{\vb{q}}+\vb{K}\vb{q}=\vb{f}\) becomes two independent scalar equations $$ \ddot {\eta}_r+\omega_r^2{\eta}_r=\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{f}\quad(r=1,2). $$ Energy splits cleanly: \(\tfrac12\vb{q}^{\T}\vb{K}\vb{q}=\tfrac12\sum_r\omega_r^2{\eta}_r^2\), with no cross terms. Each mode can be solved with the single-DOF tools of Lectures 5–19 and the answers added back through \(\vb{q}=\vb{\Phi}\vb{\eta}\).

2. Damping and forced response by modes

Take Rayleigh damping, \(\vb{C}=\alpha\vb{M}+\beta\vb{K}\). Then \(\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{C}\vb{\Phi}=\alpha\vb{I}+\beta\operatorname{diag}(\omega_r^2)\) is diagonal, and each mode has $$ \zeta_r=\frac{\alpha+\beta\omega_r^2}{2\omega_r}. $$ The receptance of mass \(i\) to a force on mass \(j\) is a sum of one-DOF terms, $$ H_{ij}(\Omega)=\sum_{r}\frac{\phi_{ir}\,\phi_{jr}}{\omega_r^2-\Omega^2+2i\zeta_r\omega_r\Omega}. $$ Switch a mode off to see what truncation costs. A dropped mode leaves a static (stiffness-like) remainder \(\phi_{ir}\phi_{jr}/\omega_r^2\) at low frequency: the "static correction". Add a damper \(c_g\) from mass 2 to ground to break proportionality and see where the diagonal modal model starts to fail (Lecture 22, nonproportional damping).

What to notice
  1. Near \(\omega_1\) the blue single-mode curve is the whole answer; near \(\omega_2\) the orange one is. Between the two resonances the denominators \(\omega_1^2-\Omega^2<0\) and \(\omega_2^2-\Omega^2>0\) have opposite signs, so the two modal terms cancel at one frequency only if the residues \(\phi_{i1}\phi_{j1}\) and \(\phi_{i2}\phi_{j2}\) have the same sign. That is always true for a driving-point receptance \(H_{ii}\), where each residue is a square \(\phi_{ir}^2>0\), so \(H_{ii}\) has an antiresonance between the modes (a minimum when damping is present). For a cross receptance the residues can have opposite signs; then the two terms have the same sign between the modes and there is no zero. At the default values \(H_{11}\) has an antiresonance (at \(\Omega=\sqrt{(k_2+k_3)/m_2}\approx1.072\), i.e. \(\Omega/\omega_2\approx0.735\)) and \(H_{21}\) does not. Section 4 shows this with arrows.
  2. Drop mode 2 and the retained curve is wrong above \(\omega_1\). It is also off at \(\Omega\to0\) by the dropped static term \(\phi_{i2}\phi_{j2}/\omega_2^2\), whose sign depends on \(i\) and \(j\). For the driving point \(H_{ii}\) that term is positive, so the truncated curve is too low. For a cross term it can be negative: at the defaults the dropped term of \(H_{21}\) is negative, so the mode-1-only curve is too high at low frequency. The static correction adds the term back.
  3. Raise \(c_g\): the exact curve leaves the modal sum. The cross term \((\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{C}\vb{\Phi})_{12}=c_g\,\phi_{21}\phi_{22}\) couples the modes, as the bookkeeping table below the plot shows.

3. Where the force goes

With the modal transformation, the force enters each modal equation through a generalized force $$ \ddot {\eta}_r+2\zeta_r\omega_r\dot {\eta}_r+\omega_r^2{\eta}_r=p_r,\qquad p_r=\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{f}. $$ This is the spatial projection of the load on the mode shape. It is a plain dot product with no \(\vb{M}\), unlike \({\eta}_r=\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{q}\) for displacements: \(\vb{M}\) converts displacements to momentum-like quantities, while \(\vb{f}\) already is the thing that \(\vb{\phi}_r\) is paired with in the virtual work \(\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{f}\). A load at a modal node (\(\phi_{jr}=0\) at the loaded mass) does not excite that mode. More generally, any \(\vb{f}\) perpendicular to \(\vb{\phi}_r\) has \(p_r=0\) and leaves mode \(r\) at rest.   3. The silence line of mode 1 is the direction of \(\vb{\phi}_1\) turned by 90° in the force plane. That is different from the \(\vb{M}\)-orthogonal complement of \(\vb{\phi}_1\) in the displacement plane, which is the direction of \(\vb{\phi}_2\).

4. Adding the modal contributions in the complex plane

At a driving frequency \(\Omega\) the receptance is a sum of two complex numbers, one per mode, $$ H_{ij}(\Omega)=\underbrace{\frac{\phi_{i1}\phi_{j1}}{\omega_1^2-\Omega^2+2i\zeta_1\omega_1\Omega}}_{\text{mode 1}}+\underbrace{\frac{\phi_{i2}\phi_{j2}}{\omega_2^2-\Omega^2+2i\zeta_2\omega_2\Omega}}_{\text{mode 2}}. $$ Draw them as arrows placed tip to tail; the arrow from the origin to the end is \(H_{ij}\). Move the \(\Omega\) slider (it also drives the plots above), or press Sweep. A term with a positive residue points to the right below its resonance, straight down at its own resonance, and to the left above it. With damping the turn is smooth; with none it is a flip. Between the resonances the mode-1 arrow has turned left and the mode-2 arrow has not, so if both residues are positive they point in opposite directions and can cancel. Use the force and response selectors under Damping and forcing in the left panel to choose \(i\) and \(j\).

5. Free response: two independent oscillators

For mass-normalized modes, initial conditions in physical coordinates transform through \(\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{M}\): $$ \vb{\eta}(0)=\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{M}\,\vb{q}(0),\qquad \dot{\vb{\eta}}(0)=\vb{\Phi}^{\T}\vb{M}\,\dot{\vb{q}}(0). $$ Each modal coordinate then solves its own single-DOF problem, \({\eta}_r(t)={\eta}_r(0)\cos\omega_rt+\dot {\eta}_r(0)\sin(\omega_rt)/\omega_r\) when undamped, and the physical motion is \(\vb{q}(t)=\vb{\Phi}\vb{\eta}(t)\). Pick an initial condition below or drag the point in the plane. Starting along a mode shape makes one \({\eta}_r\) identically zero and the masses move in lockstep; plucking one mass starts both modes.

6. Check yourself

Why are the modal components not the perpendicular projections of \(\vb{q}\) on the mode lines?

The modes are orthogonal with respect to \(\vb{M}\), not with respect to the identity. The correct projection weights the dot product by \(\vb{M}\): \({\eta}_r=\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{q}\). The two constructions agree only when \(\vb{M}\propto\vb{I}\) (which also makes the modes perpendicular).

When \(\Omega\to0\), how do the modal contributions add up to the static deflection?

Each mode contributes \(\phi_{ir}\phi_{jr}/\omega_r^2\), and the sum is \((\vb{K}^{-1})_{ij}\). Low modes with small \(\omega_r\) dominate, but the high modes still contribute a smooth static piece.

What is "nonproportional" damping geometrically?

The damping form \(\vb{q}^{\T}\vb{C}\vb{q}\) is a third ellipse whose axes do not line up with the common axes of the \(\vb{M}\) and \(\vb{K}\) ellipses. The change of axes that diagonalizes \(\vb{M}\) and \(\vb{K}\) leaves \(\vb{C}\) with off-diagonal terms, so the modes talk to each other.

Why does the force projection \(p_r=\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{f}\) contain no \(\vb{M}\), while \({\eta}_r=\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{q}\) does?

Multiply \(\vb{M}\ddot{\vb{q}}+\vb{K}\vb{q}=\vb{f}\) by \(\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\) and insert \(\vb{q}=\vb{\Phi}\vb{\eta}\). The left side becomes \(\ddot {\eta}_r+\omega_r^2{\eta}_r\) by orthonormality, and the right side is \(\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{f}\), so no \(\vb{M}\) is left on that side. The \(\vb{M}\) in \({\eta}_r=\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{q}\) comes from the same orthonormality read backwards: it is what makes \(\vb{\phi}_r^{\T}\vb{M}\vb{\Phi}\vb{\eta}={\eta}_r\).

Why can a cross receptance have no antiresonance when \(H_{ii}\) always does?

Between \(\omega_1\) and \(\omega_2\) the two modal terms have opposite denominator signs. They cancel only when the residues \(\phi_{i1}\phi_{j1}\) and \(\phi_{i2}\phi_{j2}\) share a sign. For \(i=j\) both residues are squares. For \(i\neq j\) the product \(\phi_{i1}\phi_{j1}\,\phi_{i2}\phi_{j2}\) can be negative, for example when mode 1 moves the masses in phase and mode 2 moves them out of phase.
