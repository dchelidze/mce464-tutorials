The Tuned Absorber: Antiresonance and Fixed Points

A small secondary mass, tuned to the disturbance frequency, makes the main mass stand still by pushing back with exactly the force that drives it. Adding damping trades the sharp notch for two lower peaks, and every curve passes through the same two fixed points.

1. Why the main mass stands still

A main mass \(m_1\), spring \(k_1\), carries an absorber \(m_2\) on a spring \(k_2\) and damper \(c_2\). With the force \(F\cos\Omega t\) on \(m_1\), the second equation of motion gives the absorber's force on the main mass, $$ X_2=\frac{k_2+i c_2\Omega}{k_2-m_2\Omega^2+i c_2\Omega}\,X_1 . $$ For an undamped absorber (\(c_2=0\)) at \(\Omega=\omega_a=\sqrt{k_2/m_2}\) the denominator vanishes, so \(X_1\) must vanish too: the absorber carries a finite amplitude \(X_2=-F/k_2\) and its spring force exactly cancels \(F\). Geometrically, the dynamic stiffness of the absorber becomes infinite at its own natural frequency, which is an antiresonance of the main mass, and the main mass response has a notch to zero at \(\Omega=\omega_a\).

The price is that two coupled oscillators have two resonances, one on each side of the notch. With the non-dimensional quantities $$ \mu=\frac{m_2}{m_1},\quad f=\frac{\omega_a}{\omega_1},\quad r=\frac{\Omega}{\omega_1},\quad \zeta_a=\frac{c_2}{2m_2\omega_a}, $$ the undamped resonances solve \(r^4-(1+f^2+\mu f^2)\,r^2+f^2=0\). Their separation grows like \(\sqrt{\mu}\).

Read-out
What to notice
  1. \(\zeta_a=0\): deep notch at \(r=f\) but two sharp peaks. A small detuning from \(f\) ruins the notch, so a lightly damped absorber suits a machine running at one speed.
  2. Raise \(\zeta_a\): the notch fills in, and the peaks drop. At large \(\zeta_a\) the absorber locks to the main mass and the system has a single, lower resonance \(1/\sqrt{1+\mu}\).
  3. The two peaks are equal for a particular \(f\) and \(\zeta_a\): the optimum (button). Then the curve runs level through \(P\) and \(Q\) and the amplitude is minimized over a band.
  4. Larger \(\mu\) widens the band (more separated peaks) and lowers the peaks, at the cost of mass.

2. Fixed points and the optimum

With \(\zeta_1=0\) the main-mass response is $$ \frac{X_1k_1}{F}=\frac{f^2-r^2+2i\zeta_af\,r}{(1-r^2)(f^2-r^2)-\mu f^2r^2+2i\zeta_af\,r\,(1-r^2-\mu r^2)}. $$ Two curves with different \(\zeta_a\) intersect where the magnitude does not depend on \(\zeta_a\): this happens at the two frequencies \(P,Q\) that solve \(|X_1|_{\zeta_a=0}=|X_1|_{\zeta_a=\infty}\). Den Hartog's rule picks \(f\) so that \(P\) and \(Q\) have equal height, and \(\zeta_a\) so that the curve is horizontal at the higher of them: $$ f_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{1}{1+\mu},\qquad \zeta_{a,\mathrm{opt}}=\sqrt{\frac{3\mu}{8(1+\mu)^3}},\qquad \left|\frac{X_1k_1}{F}\right|_{\max}\approx\sqrt{1+\frac{2}{\mu}}. $$

3. Check yourself

Why does a 10 % absorber on a lightly damped structure reduce the resonant amplitude from 100 to about 4.6?

With \(\mu=0.1\) the optimum gives \(\sqrt{1+2/0.1}=\sqrt{21}\approx4.6\). The energy the main mass would store at resonance now flows into the absorber and is dissipated there, so the peak is set by \(\mu\), not by the structure's own damping.

An undamped absorber is tuned to 50 Hz. What happens if the machine speed drifts to 55 Hz?

The notch is narrow, so the main mass is no longer stationary, and 55 Hz may lie near the upper resonance. The fix is damping (a wider, shallower notch) or a self-tuning absorber.

How does this relate to isolation (Tutorial 5)?

An isolator reduces transmitted force above \(r=\sqrt2\) but offers nothing at resonance. An absorber works at a chosen frequency, even at resonance. They are used together: isolate at high speeds, absorb at the running-speed resonance.
