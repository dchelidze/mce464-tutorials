The Tuned Absorber: Antiresonance, Fixed Points and Stroke

A small secondary mass, tuned to the disturbance frequency, makes the main mass stand still by pushing back with exactly the force that drives it. Adding damping trades the sharp notch for two lower peaks, every curve passes through the same two fixed points, and the absorber pays for all of it with its own stroke.

1. Why the main mass stands still

A main mass \(m_1\), spring \(k_1\), carries an absorber \(m_2\) on a spring \(k_2\) and damper \(c_2\). With the force \(F\cos\Omega t\) on \(m_1\), the second equation of motion relates the two amplitudes, $$ X_2=\frac{k_2+i c_2\Omega}{k_2-m_2\Omega^2+i c_2\Omega}\,X_1 . $$ For an undamped absorber (\(c_2=0\)) at \(\Omega=\omega_a=\sqrt{k_2/m_2}\) the denominator vanishes, so \(X_1\) must vanish too: the absorber carries a finite amplitude \(X_2=-F/k_2\) and its spring force exactly cancels \(F\). The dynamic stiffness of the absorber becomes infinite at its own natural frequency, which is an antiresonance of the main mass.

The price is that two coupled oscillators have two resonances, one on each side of the notch, and that the absorber must move. With $$ \mu=\frac{m_2}{m_1},\quad f=\frac{\omega_a}{\omega_1},\quad r=\frac{\Omega}{\omega_1},\quad \zeta_a=\frac{c_2}{2m_2\omega_a},\quad \zeta_1=\frac{c_1}{2m_1\omega_1}, $$ the undamped resonances solve \(r^4-(1+f^2+\mu f^2)\,r^2+f^2=0\), and at the notch the absorber stroke is $$ \left|X_2-X_1\right|\frac{k_1}{F}=\frac{k_1}{k_2}=\frac{1}{\mu f^2}. $$ A light absorber needs a long stroke: at \(\mu=0.1\), \(f=1\) the spring deflects ten times the static deflection of the main structure.

Read-out
What to notice
  1. \(\zeta_a=0\): deep notch at \(r=f\) but two sharp peaks. A small detuning from \(f\) ruins the notch, so a lightly damped absorber suits a machine running at one speed.
  2. Raise \(\zeta_a\): the notch fills in and the peaks drop. At large \(\zeta_a\) the absorber locks to the main mass and the system has a single, lower resonance \(1/\sqrt{1+\mu}\).
  3. Press Den Hartog optimum: the two peaks become nearly equal and the curve runs almost level through \(P\) and \(Q\). Compare the numerical peak with \(\sqrt{1+2/\mu}\) in the read-out.
  4. Watch the orange stroke curve. Lower \(\mu\) means a longer stroke at the notch, \(1/(\mu f^2)\); damping cuts the stroke along with the peaks.
  5. Raise \(\zeta_1\) and press Numerical optimum: with main-mass damping the fixed points no longer exist, and the best tuning moves below \(1/(1+\mu)\).

2. Fixed points and the optimum

With \(\zeta_1=0\) the main-mass response is $$ \frac{X_1k_1}{F}=\frac{f^2-r^2+2i\zeta_af\,r}{(1-r^2)(f^2-r^2)-\mu f^2r^2+2i\zeta_af\,r\,(1-r^2-\mu r^2)}. $$ Two curves with different \(\zeta_a\) intersect where the magnitude does not depend on \(\zeta_a\): this happens at the two frequencies \(P,Q\) that solve \(|X_1|_{\zeta_a=0}=|X_1|_{\zeta_a=\infty}\). Den Hartog's rule picks \(f\) so that \(P\) and \(Q\) have equal height, and \(\zeta_a\) so that the curve is nearly horizontal there: $$ f_{\mathrm{opt}}=\frac{1}{1+\mu},\qquad \zeta_{a,\mathrm{opt}}=\sqrt{\frac{3\mu}{8(1+\mu)}},\qquad \left|\frac{X_1k_1}{F}\right|_{P,Q}=\sqrt{1+\frac{2}{\mu}}. $$ The last value is the common height of \(P\) and \(Q\); the true peak of the optimized curve is slightly higher, because the curve cannot be level at both points with one \(\zeta_a\).

Check the reference frequency. The damping ratio above is referred to the absorber's own frequency \(\omega_a\). If it is referred to the main system instead, \(\zeta'=c_2/(2m_2\omega_1)=f\,\zeta_a\), and the same optimum reads \(\zeta'_{\mathrm{opt}}=\sqrt{3\mu/(8(1+\mu)^3)}\). Both forms appear in textbooks; mixing them detunes the design (at \(\mu=0.25\) the peak rises from 3.01 to 3.27).

3. Main-mass damping and the numerical optimum

When \(\zeta_1>0\) the curves for different \(\zeta_a\) no longer share fixed points, and Den Hartog's closed form is only a starting guess. The Numerical optimum button minimizes the largest value of \(|X_1|k_1/F\) over \(r\) by a pattern search in \((f,\zeta_a)\), starting from Den Hartog's values. As \(\zeta_1\) grows, the optimal tuning falls below \(1/(1+\mu)\) and the gain from the absorber shrinks, because the structure already dissipates part of the energy.

4. Check yourself

Why does a 10 % absorber on a lightly damped structure reduce the resonant amplitude from 100 to about 4.6?

With \(\mu=0.1\) the optimum gives \(\sqrt{1+2/0.1}=\sqrt{21}\approx4.58\) at the fixed points, and the computed peak is 4.59. The energy the main mass would store at resonance now flows into the absorber and is dissipated there, so the peak is set by \(\mu\), not by the structure's own damping.

An undamped absorber with \(\mu=0.1\) is tuned to the running speed, \(f=1\). How far does its spring deflect, compared with the static deflection \(F/k_1\)?

\(|X_2-X_1|k_1/F=1/(\mu f^2)=10\). The absorber spring must survive ten times the static deflection of the main structure, and fatigue of that spring often limits the design before the mass does.

An undamped absorber is tuned to 50 Hz. What happens if the machine speed drifts to 55 Hz?

The notch is narrow, so the main mass is no longer stationary, and 55 Hz may lie near the upper resonance. The fix is damping (a wider, shallower notch) or a self-tuning absorber.

How does this relate to isolation (Tutorial 5)?

An isolator reduces transmitted force above \(r=\sqrt2\) but offers nothing at resonance. An absorber works at a chosen frequency, even at resonance, but leaves the high-frequency transmission almost unchanged: the threshold where \(|F_T|=F\) stays within a few percent of \(\sqrt2\) (compare the two curves in the last figure). They are used together: isolate at high speeds, absorb at the running-speed resonance.
