Beam Modes: Boundary Conditions, Spectrum and Modal Projection

A beam replaces the coordinate vector with a displacement field \(w(x,t)\). The four end conditions decide which shapes are admissible and therefore which frequencies exist. Once the modes are known, projection turns a distributed load into one generalized force per mode, and a few modes often suffice. This page shows both steps and checks each against an independent result.

1. Boundary conditions define the spectrum

For free vibration of a uniform Euler–Bernoulli beam, \(w(x,t)=\phi(x)\,q(t)\) separates the beam equation into \(\ddot q+\omega^2q=0\) and the spatial eigenproblem $$ EI\,\phi''''-\rho A\,\omega^2\phi=0,\qquad \beta^4=\frac{\rho A\,\omega^2}{EI}, $$ whose general solution is $$ \phi(x)=a_1\sin\beta x+a_2\cos\beta x+a_3\sinh\beta x+a_4\cosh\beta x. $$ Each end supplies two conditions: clamped \(w=w'=0\), pinned \(w=w''=0\), free \(w''=w'''=0\). The four conditions are linear in \(\vb{a}=[a_1\ a_2\ a_3\ a_4]\T\), so they read \(\vb{B}(\beta L)\,\vb{a}=\vb{0}\). A nontrivial shape needs $$ \det\vb{B}(\beta L)=0, $$ the frequency equation. Its roots \(\beta_nL\) give $$ \omega_n=(\beta_nL)^2\sqrt{\frac{EI}{\rho A L^4}}. $$ The page builds \(\vb{B}\) from the end conditions you pick, finds the roots \(\beta_nL\) numerically, and takes each mode shape from the null vector of \(\vb{B}(\beta_nL)\). For pinned–pinned ends the determinant reduces to \(\sin\beta L=0\), so \(\beta_nL=n\pi\) and \(\phi_n=\sin(n\pi x/L)\), as on slide 8.

Orthogonality as cancelling areas

Slide 10's orthogonality conditions are integrals over the span: \(\int_0^L\rho A\,\phi_m\phi_n\,dx=0\) and \(\int_0^L EI\,\phi_m''\phi_n''\,dx=0\) for \(m\neq n\). Geometrically, the product curve encloses as much area above the axis as below it. The integration by parts behind this works only because both modes satisfy the same end conditions, so the boundary terms vanish. Change the end conditions and the cancellation survives for every pair. For \(m=n\) the stiffness integral over the mass integral is the Rayleigh quotient, \(\int\phi_n''^2d\xi\big/\int\phi_n^2d\xi=(\beta_nL)^4\) with \(\xi=x/L\).

What to notice
  1. The cantilever (clamped–free) starts at \(\beta_1L=1.8751\), and \(\omega_2/\omega_1=6.267\). The pinned–pinned ratios are exactly \(n^2\) (4, 9, 16, …).
  2. Clamped–clamped and free–free share their nonzero roots \((4.7300, 7.8532,\ldots)\). Clamped–pinned and pinned–free share theirs too \((3.9266, 7.0686,\ldots)\). Turn on the curvature to see why: the curvature of one is the shape of the other (question 1 below).
  3. Count the nodes. Clamped–free mode \(n\) has \(n-1\) interior nodes, pinned–free has \(n\), and free–free has \(n+1\). The rigid-body modes take the lower node counts: the rotation about a pin has none, a free–free translation none and its rotation one.
  4. For large \(n\) every row of the ladder approaches equal spacing \(\pi\). Away from the ends the shapes look like sinusoids, and the hyperbolic terms only matter near the ends.

2. Projecting a load onto the modes

Take the simply supported beam of slide 8 and expand \(w(x,t)=\sum_n\phi_n(x)\,q_n(t)\) with \(\phi_n=\sin(n\pi x/L)\). Premultiplying by \(\phi_n\) and integrating over the span (slide 11) gives $$ m_n=\int_0^L\rho A\,\phi_n^2\,dx=\frac{\rho AL}{2},\qquad k_n=\int_0^L EI\,\phi_n''^2\,dx=\frac{EI L}{2}\Big(\frac{n\pi}{L}\Big)^4, $$ and the generalized force is $$ Q_n=\int_0^L\phi_n\,p\,dx. $$ A point load \(F\) at \(x=a\) gives \(Q_n=F\sin(n\pi a/L)\). A uniform load \(p_0\) gives \(Q_n=p_0L\,(1-\cos n\pi)/(n\pi)\), which vanishes for every even mode. With modal damping \(c_n=2\zeta m_n\omega_n\), the truncated response is slide 12's sum $$ w_N(x,\Omega)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\phi_n(x)\,Q_n}{k_n-m_n\Omega^2+i\,c_n\Omega}, $$ and for the bending moment $$ M_N(x,\Omega)=-EI\,w_N''=\sum_{n=1}^{N}EI\Big(\frac{n\pi}{L}\Big)^2\frac{\phi_n(x)\,Q_n}{k_n-m_n\Omega^2+i\,c_n\Omega}. $$ Independent check. At \(\Omega=0\) the exact static solution is known: \(w=Fbx(L^2-b^2-x^2)/(6EIL)\) for \(x\le a\) with \(b=L-a\), and \(w=p_0x(L^3-2Lx^2+x^3)/(24EI)\) for the uniform load. The moments are \(M=Fbx/L\) and \(M=p_0x(L-x)/2\). The dark reference curve below is this exact static solution plus the dynamic part of 200 modes, \(\sum\phi_nQ_n[H_n(\Omega)-H_n(0)]\), which converges far faster than the plain modal sum.

What to notice
  1. Point load and output both at midspan: one mode gives 98.55% of the exact static deflection \(FL^3/48EI\). The same single mode gives only 81.06% of the midspan moment \(FL/4\). Eight modes still give only 94.96%. Moment terms fall off like \(1/n^2\), deflection terms like \(1/n^4\).
  2. With the defaults \((a=0.3L,\ x=0.7L)\) one mode overshoots: 109.25% of the exact static deflection. Two modes give 99.82%. Mode 2 enters with a negative sign and corrects the estimate.
  3. Choose the uniform load: \(Q_2=Q_4=\dots=0\). A symmetric load cannot drive antisymmetric modes, and \(N=2\) adds nothing over \(N=1\).
  4. Below \(\omega_1\) the one-mode curve tracks the reference closely. Near \(\omega_2\) it misses a whole resonance unless \(Q_2\) or \(\phi_2(x)\) is zero. This is slide 12's condition: the load and the output must both couple strongly to mode 1.

3. Check yourself

Why do clamped–clamped and free–free beams have the same nonzero \(\beta_nL\)?

Let \(\phi\) be a clamped–clamped mode and set \(\psi=\phi''\). Then \(\psi''=\phi''''=\beta^4\phi\) and \(\psi'''=\beta^4\phi'\). Both vanish at clamped ends, so \(\psi\) satisfies the free–free conditions with the same \(\beta\). The argument also runs backwards. Both frequency equations reduce to \(\cos\beta L\cosh\beta L=1\). The same argument maps clamped–pinned onto free–pinned, so both satisfy \(\tan\beta L=\tanh\beta L\). On this page the curvature of the clamped–clamped mode and the free–free mode agree to about \(10^{-15}\).

Free–free beams have two rigid-body modes at \(\omega=0\). What does orthogonality to them say about the elastic modes?

The rigid modes are a translation \(\phi=1\) and a rotation \(\phi=x\). Mass orthogonality gives \(\int_0^L\rho A\,\phi_n\,dx=0\) and \(\int_0^L\rho A\,x\,\phi_n\,dx=0\). An elastic mode carries no net momentum and no net angular momentum, so a free beam vibrating in its elastic modes does not drift. A pinned–free beam has only the rotation about the pin. For the six elastic modes computed here both integrals are below \(10^{-9}\), which is the quadrature error.

How can one mode overestimate the static deflection by 9%?

The mode-2 term is \(\sin(2\pi a/L)\sin(2\pi x/L)\,F/k_2\). For \(a=0.3L\) and \(x=0.7L\) the two sines are \(0.951\) and \(-0.951\), so the term is negative. Truncating at \(N=1\) drops a negative correction. Modal sums are not monotone in \(N\) unless every term has the same sign, as it does when the load and the output are at the same point.

Why does the bending moment need more modes than the deflection?

\(M=-EIw''\) multiplies each modal term by \((n\pi/L)^2\), and statically \(Q_n/k_n\propto n^{-4}\). The deflection terms therefore fall like \(n^{-4}\) and the moment terms only like \(n^{-2}\). Under a point load the exact moment diagram has a kink, and a sum of smooth sines converges slowly at a kink. Stress follows the moment, so a model that is accurate for deflection can still be poor for stress. This is slide 14's point that convergence is judged for the selected output.

The determinant is divided by \(\cosh\beta L\). Does that change the frequencies?

No. \(\cosh\beta L>0\), so the zeros are unchanged. Without the division, entries of \(\vb{B}\) grow like \(e^{\beta L}\) and the curve becomes unreadable after the first root. The division is a scaling choice, not physics.
